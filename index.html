Project overview

What the analysis is trying to explain

The project focuses on two questions: why highway fuel use differs across states, and how road transportation fits into total transportation CO₂ emissions.

In 2024, road travel per resident was close to its 2000 level, while reported highway fuel use per 1,000 vehicle miles was lower. That points to lower fuel use per mile, rather than a sustained decline in travel, as the larger long-run contributor to lower highway fuel use per resident.

2024 fuel note: FHWA's 2024 MF-21 documentation warns that revised estimation procedures and state reporting changes can affect comparability with prior years. We show 2024 as the latest observation, but rely more heavily on 2000–2023 for structural comparisons.

Project logic

The analysis in three steps

1

Separate travel from fuel intensity

Use VMT per resident to measure how much road travel occurs. Fuel intensity is highway fuel use per 1,000 VMT: how many gallons of reported highway fuel are used for a given amount of road travel. We use fuel intensity rather than MPG because MPG is a vehicle-level efficiency measure, while this analysis compares statewide total highway fuel with statewide total VMT across all vehicle types and travel conditions.

2

Use vehicle mix as context

Combine VM-4 vehicle percentages with VM-2 road VMT to estimate statewide light-truck and heavy-truck shares. Vehicle mix explains some, but not most, of the differences in fuel intensity.

3

Connect roads to transportation CO₂

Convert FHWA highway fuel to an on-road CO₂ proxy and compare it with EIA's broader transportation-sector CO₂ estimate.

Road travel and highway fuel | Main findings

What the road-travel and fuel results show

Nationally, lower fuel use per resident is mostly a fuel-intensity story, not a sustained decline in travel.

From 2000 to 2023, VMT per resident changed very little (about −1%), while highway fuel use per 1,000 VMT fell about 7.8%. Highway fuel use per resident fell about 8.7%. In other words, the long-run decline in fuel use per person came mainly from using less fuel for a given amount of road travel.

The sharp drop in 2020 was different: it was primarily a temporary travel shock. VMT per resident fell about 11.9% from 2019 to 2020 and then recovered substantially.

State differences come from different combinations of travel and fuel intensity. Wyoming is a clear example of both high VMT and high fuel intensity. California is low on both measures. Indiana has high VMT but comparatively low fuel intensity, while Alaska has lower VMT but high fuel intensity. The quadrant chart is useful for identifying these different pathways; it is not a scorecard.

Vehicle mix helps explain some of the fuel-intensity differences. States with larger light-truck and heavy-truck shares tend to use more fuel per 1,000 VMT, but the relationship is only partial. In 2023, light- and heavy-truck shares together explained about 22% of the cross-state variation in fuel intensity.

Bottom line: highway fuel use per resident is best understood as the result of two separate drivers: how much road travel occurs and how fuel-intensive that travel is. States differ in which of those drivers matters more, so one metric alone does not describe the full transportation picture.

Supporting Road travel and highway fuel analysis | Vehicle mix

Do pickups, SUVs, and heavy trucks explain fuel intensity?

FHWA VM-4 gives the vehicle mix within Interstate, Other Arterials, and Other roads, separately for rural and urban travel. We weight those percentages by actual road VMT from VM-2 to estimate statewide vehicle shares.

In 2023, the combined light- and heavy-truck share had a moderate relationship with gallons per 1,000 VMT (correlation about 0.45). Light- and heavy-truck shares together explained about 22% of the cross-state variation. Vehicle mix matters, but it is not the whole explanation.

This chart uses 2023, a complete VM-4 year before the 2024 MF-21 comparability concern. VM-4 does not isolate pickup trucks from SUVs and vans; they are grouped as light trucks.

Transportation emissions | Transportation CO₂

How does highway fuel use fit into total transportation emissions?

EIA SEDS transportation-sector CO₂ includes the broader transportation system. We compare it with an on-road proxy derived from FHWA MF-21 highway gasoline and special fuel. Gasoline is converted using 8,887 g CO₂/gallon and special fuel using 10,180 g CO₂/gallon as a diesel proxy, following the EPA factors used in this project.

The FHWA result is an approximation, not an inventory. FHWA “special fuel” is not pure diesel, and the historical project series uses private/commercial highway special fuel for consistency. The ratio to EIA transportation CO₂ is therefore a reconciliation measure, not a literal emissions share.

Percentage comparison: the on-road proxy divided by EIA total transportation CO₂ shows how large the highway-fuel-based estimate is relative to the broader transportation total. For example, 87.8% means the proxy is equivalent to about 88% of EIA transportation CO₂. The remaining percentage should not be interpreted as an exact non-road share because the two series have different scopes and accounting methods.

How to read Transportation emissions

National transportation CO₂ was 1,863.6 million metric tons in 2024, about 7.8% below the 2007 peak. Per-capita transportation CO₂ was 5.48 metric tons, about 18% below 2000. The on-road proxy equaled 87.8% of EIA transportation CO₂ in 2023 and 84.6% in 2024.

Low ratios in states such as Alaska, Hawaii, Louisiana, and Washington can reflect a larger role for aviation, marine, pipeline, or other non-road transportation. Ratios above 100% are accounting and scope mismatches; they are not evidence of negative non-road emissions.

Why VM-4 and VM-2 are combined

VM-4 percentages sum to 100% within each road group. We do not average those percentages. We multiply each VM-4 vehicle percentage by the matching VM-2 VMT for that road group, then sum the estimated vehicle VMT across rural and urban road groups. This gives a statewide VMT-weighted vehicle mix.

What “highway fuel per 1,000 VMT” means

Reported FHWA highway fuel divided by public-road VMT. It is a system-level fuel-intensity measure. It can reflect fleet efficiency, vehicle mix, freight, road conditions, travel patterns, and differences in fuel/VMT accounting. It is not passenger-vehicle MPG.

What the 2024 caution means

FHWA's 2024 MF-21 notes revised estimation procedures for public and non-highway use and warns that some state data may not be comparable with prior years because of reporting or analysis changes. Our highway-fuel series also shows a larger-than-usual national change in 2024. We therefore show 2024 but avoid making strong structural claims from the one-year movement.

Why the on-road CO₂ ratio can exceed 100%

The numerator and denominator come from different systems with different fuel definitions, allocation methods, revisions, and sector boundaries. A value above 100% is a reconciliation flag. We do not cap it and do not interpret it as a literal physical share.

EIA methodology and variable definitions are documented in the SEDS technical notes.
